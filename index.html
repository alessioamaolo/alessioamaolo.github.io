Research

I am interested in the physics of nanophotonic design. While inverse-designed or computationally optimized structures have seen remarkable success in manipulating light on small length-scales, the underlying physical mechanisms (and the absolute limits of what is achievable) are not fully understood. I am generally interested in accurately modeling photonic systems and exploring what Maxwell’s equations ultimately allow. My ultimate goal is to build a unified theoretical framework for designing, benchmarking, and fabricating nanophotonic devices in realistic engineering contexts.

These are a few of my recent interests:

Fundamental limits in photonics

Inverse design, or computational optimization, can produce devices that are counter-intuitive but high-performing. This is a bottom-up approach: it produces feasible devices without answering whether they are truly optimal, or if expanding our search efforts would yield even greater performance. To answer this, we use a top-down approach: given a problem with certain constraints (area, material, etc.), what is the absolute highest performance physically achievable? These limits provide rigorous performance benchmarks: they certify optimality, predict where performance gains can be found, and can mathematically prove the suboptimality of certain engineering choices.

Over the last few years, we have developed this methodology across a wide variety of contexts, from nonlinear scattering to information-capacity objectives.

Certifying optimality is only one aspect of these bounds. First, they can predict physical scaling laws with surprising accuracy. Second, and perhaps most surprisingly, they are information-bearing: limits contain structural data which can be extracted to initialize traditional inverse design algorithms.

My recent interests in this area are two-fold:

  1. Developing new inverse design algorithms. How can we extract structural information from limits to perform better structural optimization? This involves applying optimization-theory machinery to theorize about how optimal structural features can be extracted, as well as advancing the underlying numerics. Our codebase represents the state-of-the-art for calculating fundamental limits in photonics; we are currently expanding it to handle 3D scattering problems for realistic engineering scenarios, alongside developing new numerical algorithms for these generalized limit frameworks.
  2. Understanding fundamental physics. With the recent maturing of limits methodology, what can limits tell us about what is possible via structuring for a variety of relevant physics problems? Currently I am working on broadband heat transfer, Purcell enhancement, and broadband scattered power.

Information-theoretic photonics

Recently, I have been interested in how we treat information-theoretic objectives in photonics. Information theory is the foundation of communication, sensing, and imaging. However, when addressing these topics, which inherently rely on physical photonic devices like antennas, sensors, and camera front-ends, we typically optimize proxy metrics such as signal-to-noise ratio, resolution, power transfer, or the number of available modes, rather than the information capacity itself.

I argue that we should instead optimize photonic structures directly for information-theoretic objectives. In our recent work on the maximum Shannon capacity of photonic structures, we demonstrated how information theory can be explicitly linked to physical structural design. This framework allowed us to derive an expression for the photonic Shannon capacity (the absolute maximum data rate for a given photonic communication problem) and simultaneously co-optimize the material structure alongside the power allocation of each channel. Applying the bounding methodologies discussed above, we were then able to establish rigorous limits on Shannon capacity across all possible photonic structures.

In recent research focusing on optically incoherent photonic mutual information, we explored how this optimization problem shifts when source control is restricted and channel laws become more complex. Specifically, we examined scenarios highly relevant to near-field sensing and imaging where the receiver cannot measure the optical phase, the sources are incoherent, or both. While these present significantly tougher information-theoretic challenges, they yield precise conclusions regarding how near-field sensors and imaging equipment should be structured.

This research is in its early stages. I’m broadly interested in:

  • How can realistic information-theoretic photonic problems be rigorously mathematically defined?
  • How can we efficiently apply inverse design to these specific objectives, and how do the resulting structures perform compared to those derived from standard proxy objectives?
  • How can these formulations benefit realistic engineering scenarios? For instance, could an information-theoretic approach yield strictly superior near-field sensors, or enable the design of ultra-high-bandwidth silicon photonic communication devices?
  • Can we apply a limits formulation to calculate fundamental bounds on information-theoretic quantities? Similar to bounds on conventional metrics like the Purcell factor, are these limits tight enough to supply structural information for device design?

Code

Everything lives on my GitHub.

dolphindes

A recent library for calculation of photonic device performance bounds. I am a core contributor / maintainer. Most of my newer publications use Dolphindes to some extent, and I use it as a repository for examples reproducing my results.

fluxmax

A highly parallelizable (via JAX SPMD distributed array sharding) optimization library for designing photonic metasurfaces for near-field radiative heat transfer (NFRHT). Still a bit sparse, but there will be a lot more code and examples soon.

photonic_gearbox

Implements the eigenvalue-accelerated LDOS optimization in 2D polar coordinates to design compact ultra-high quality cavities. (Repo is still not public but will be soon.)
